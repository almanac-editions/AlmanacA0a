The multiplicative grid

A polynomial can take whole-number values everywhere you look and still not have whole-number coefficients. This is the story of one small theorem that says exactly which polynomials do that on a geometric grid — and of how far that theorem has been checked.

The result below was proved, machine-verified, and then edited into a form a stranger can check. It is a small, easy theorem, and the page says so plainly further down. What is unusual is that every claim on it carries a record of what warrants it.

The oldest version of the question

Take the polynomial x(x−1)/2. Its coefficients are halves — not whole numbers — yet at every integer it returns an integer: 0, 0, 1, 3, 6, 10. The halving always cancels, because among any two consecutive integers one is even.

So "has integer coefficients" and "takes integer values" are different properties, and the second is strictly weaker. Pólya's theorem (1915) says exactly how much weaker: the polynomials taking integer values at every integer are precisely the integer combinations of the binomial polynomials (xk). The failure of integrality in the coefficients is entirely accounted for by changing basis. Everything below is a deformation of that sentence.

What this record proves

Replace the grid. Instead of asking for integrality at 0, 1, 2, 3, …, ask for it along a geometric progression — at 1,q,q2,q3,…, and in both directions, at negative powers too. Replace the coefficients as well: they are no longer numbers but expressions in two independent variables.

Nev=∑u∈Z∑r≥0AtYˇuνr(Yˇ)

In words: the Laurent polynomials taking values in At at every point of the multiplicative grid are exactly the At-span of the divided classes. Same shape as Pólya, one deformation further out.

Reading the symbols

v, t
two independent variables, and q=v2. The variable t is a free parameter that rides through the whole argument without ever being constrained.
At=Z[v±1,t±1]
the coefficients we want values to land in — whole-number combinations of powers of v and t, positive or negative.
Ft=Q(v)[t±1]
the larger ring the polynomials are actually written in. Fractions are allowed here. This gap is the whole theorem.
Yˇ
the grid coordinate. The grid is the set of points Yˇ=qχ for every whole number χ, positive and negative.
νr
the divided classes: ν0=1, and νr(X)=∏s=0r−1X−qsqr−qs. These play the part the binomial polynomials play in Pólya's theorem.
Nev
the even Newton lattice — everything grid-integral.

Where the difficulty lives

The polynomials have coefficients in Ft; what is required to lie in At are their values. If the coefficients were already in At there would be nothing to prove — the values would follow for free. The content is that a polynomial can fail the coefficient test and pass the value test, and that the divided classes account for every way this can happen.

One element shows the gap is real. Take the first divided class:

ν1(Yˇ)=Yˇ−1q−1

Its single coefficient is 1/(q−1), which is not in At — q−1 is not invertible there. But watch what it does on the grid:

The first six rows are the grid. The fraction always cancels, in both directions — which is why ν1 belongs to Nev despite its coefficient. The last row is not a grid point, and there the cancellation fails; that row is the subject of the third witness below. Three ways the statement is tight

Every clause in the statement is doing work, and one value each shows it. These are the sharp edges of the result: what it delivers, what it stops short of, and the hypothesis it cannot do without.

The divided classes are genuinely needed

ν1 is grid-integral and its coefficient is not integral. It lies in Nev — the table above shows it landing in At at every node — while its single coefficient (q−1)−1 lies outside At. So Nev is strictly larger than At[Yˇ±1], and the theorem says something the coefficients alone do not.

It spans, and stops there

The generating family satisfies a relation: (q−1)ν1(Yˇ)−Yˇ+1=0 over At. So the divided classes span Nev without being independent, and this is a spanning theorem — a basis theorem would be a stronger result and is not this one. Whether Nev is free over At on some other family is open.

The grid must be even

At the odd node v3 the value leaves At: ν1(v3)=v3−1v2−1=v2+v+1v+1. Since q=v2, the grid is the even powers v2χ, and ν1 is integral at every one of them — but refine the grid to all powers of v and integrality breaks at the first new node. The word even in the statement is a hypothesis, not a convenience.

Each of these is settled by a single value, which is worth saying plainly: one value is a complete proof of a statement of this shape, not evidence for it. Nothing here rests on a sample.

Where the statement came from

Every mathematical statement here is a known result, reached along a line worth setting out, because each step changed what the question was about.

  1. 1915

    Pólya — the additive origin

    Integer-valued polynomials on the integers are the Z-span of the binomial polynomials. The template for everything that follows.

  2. 1933 · 1990

    Gel'fond, then Gramain — moving the grid

    Ask for integrality along 1,q,q2,… instead. For entire functions that is Gel'fond, 1933 — the multiplicative analogue of Pólya's problem. For polynomials it is Gramain's Proposition 2.2: for an integer q≥2, the polynomials integral at every qk are generated by the Gauss binomial polynomials — which are exactly the divided classes of this record.

  3. 2016

    Harman and Hopkins — deforming the coefficients

    Which P∈Q(q)[x] satisfy P([n]q)∈Z[q]? Their §1 answers it on the additive grid of q-integers; their §4 takes the nodes over all of Z, which is the bilateral form this record needs. Their §4 also contains the remark that joins the two lines: setting z=1+(q−1)x turns evaluation at x=[n]q into evaluation at z=qn — the additive grid becomes the geometric one, node for node.

  4. this record

    What is added — two routine transports

    The grid variable is inverted, so the objects are Laurent rather than polynomial; at a grid of units that is a clearing argument, not a difficulty. And the coefficients carry a free parameter t, which arrives from the setting the goal was posed in and rides through as a spectator — the integrality test decomposes coefficient-wise. No predecessor is located for that parameter, and three independent sources record its absence.

How far it has been checked

The record exists in three layers — a written proof, a Julia instrument that computes the objects, and a Lean development the proof kernel accepts. The grades below are different kinds of claim, each meaning exactly what it states, and the edition keeps them distinct.

A Lean declaration states the claim and its axiom base is exactly propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound — no sorryAx, no custom axiom. Every one of these was measured first-hand through the Lean language server.

The computed rows are the instrument (acceptance battery 87/87, with the grid integrality checked for r in 0…8 against χ in −9…9, and a negative control of 249 cases required to fail — which they do) and an independent oracle referee pin (verdict PROVED_BOUNDED, 60 positive and 5 hostile seeds, 0 disagreements).

The deliverable is the theorem with a clean axiom base. No theory of integer-valued polynomials, classical or q-deformed, exists in Mathlib — not Pólya's theorem, not a q-analogue. That absence is why the formal layer had to be built rather than cited.

The honest size of the result

At GL1 this is the degenerate case, and the edition says so first. The root system is empty, so the algebra is commutative and its centre is all of it; the Weyl group is trivial; the Harish–Chandra projection is the identity map. Two of the three clauses in the original goal are formalities at this rank. The value of the record is that a small true statement was carried end to end, and that the cost and the failure modes of doing so were measured. Rank 2 and above are open, and nothing here settles them.

A tested agreement is not a proof. The Julia and Lean layers are two independent implementations joined by a shared naming discipline, not by a checked morphism. Agreement between them is established by testing, over the bounds printed above, and it licenses nothing beyond them.

The ancestral result is not our result. The Harman–Hopkins bilateral quantum Pólya theorem is the ancestor of the transported argument. The reconciliation that would identify their lattice with this one is open.

What is still open

Three things, stated because an edition that lists only its results teaches a false boundary: whether Nev is free over At on some family other than the dependent one above; the four-way lattice reconciliation with the Harman–Hopkins statement; and the rank ≥ 2 cases, where the two clauses that are formalities here carry real weight and the argument does not obviously transport.
